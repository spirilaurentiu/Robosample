# **Brownian dynamics study of a polymer chain of linked rigid bodies<sup>8</sup> )**

M. R. Pear and J. H. Weiner

*Department of Physics and Division of Engineering, Brown University, Providence, Rhode Island 02912*  (Received 2 March 1979; accepted 2 April 1979)

A Brownian dynamics model for the backbone chain of a macromolecule is developed as a system of linked rigid bodies so that constraints on valence angles and bond lengths are satisfied exactly. For comparison, a corresponding flexible model is developed in which bond lengths and valence angles are held nearly constant by strong harmonic potentials. Equilibrium properties and barrier crossing rates are examined theoretically and by computer simulation of both models, with differences arising due to the presence of constraints in the rigid case. A compensating potential based on the metric determinant of unconstrained coordinates in the rigid model is found to eliminate the effect of constraints. Barrier crossing rates in the transition state approximation are studied when a force fixed in space is applied to the end atoms of the three-bond chain. An exact transition state rate formula developed for this case predicts curved Arrhenius plots of barrier crossing rates; this result is confirmed by computer simulation.

### **I. INTRODUCTION**

One of the principal characteristics of long-chain molecules, either when in solution or when part of an amorphous network, is the ease in transition between their many possible conformations since rotation about backbone bonds is opposed only by relatively low energy barriers. The equilibrium distribution among the various conformations accessible to a given chain has been studied extensively, but the detailed investigation of the kinetic aspects of the process, relating rates of transitions to chain characteristics and barrier heights, has been undertaken intensively only recently.

One of the early papers treating this question from the viewpoint of the theory of thermally activated rate processes is that of Helfand. 1 Under certain simplifying assumptions, he developed an extension of Kramers' rate theory in an idealization of the barrier crossing problem in long-chain molecules. More recently, there have been a number of studies of this process using computer simulation techniques in which the thermal aspects of the process are modeled by use of the Langevin equation. In this equation, a systematic damping force and a random fluctuating force are added to the normal equations of motion, which then become, for a particle of mass *m* in a potential U,

$$m\ddot{\mathbf{x}} = -\nabla U - \eta \, \dot{\mathbf{x}} + \mathbf{L}(t) , \qquad (1.1)$$

where 17 is the viscosity and L(t) is the fluctuating Langevin force. The Langevin force satisfies the statistical relations

$$\langle L_i(t) \rangle = 0 ,$$

$$\langle L_i(t)L_j(t') \rangle = 2\eta k_B T \delta(t - t') \delta_{ij} , \qquad (1.2)$$

where *T* is the temperature, the *L;* are the Cartesian

components of L, and ( ) represents an average over an ensemble of particles, governed by Eq. (1.1). The term Brownian dynamics has been introduced2 to describe computer simulations based on the Langevin equation as opposed to molecular dynamics simulations in which the many degrees of freedom of the heat bath are simulated explicitly as a collection of particles interacting with specified force laws.

Weiner and Peara studied an idealized model by this method. In this model, we have a linear chain of atoms, each constrained to move in a Single direction, with nearest neighbors subject to a double-well potential which was intended to mimic rotational energy barriers. Particular attention was devoted in this study to the effect of a stress applied to the end of the chain on rates of barrier crossings; good agreement was found between computer simulation results and equilibrium distributions as predicted by equilibrium statistical mechanics. The same idealized one-dimensional model has been studied with the Brownian dynamics technique by Helfand, 4 who has employed more sophisticated numerical techniques for both the numerical treatment of the Langevin equation and the statistical analysiS of the numerical results. He finds that the Kramers rate expression he developed earlier! fits the numerical results well and that cooperative barrier crossings playa relatively minor role in conformational transitions.

More realistic models of long-chain molecules with fixed bond lengths and valence angles have been studied by computer simulation within the Langevin framework by Fixman. 5 His primary concern is with the highviscosity regime, so that inertia terms are neglected. He uses his model to study mode and conformational relaxation rates in this approximation.

In this paper, we present some initial results using a computer program for the simulation of a long-chain molecule with fixed bond lengths and valence angles. Our approach and emphasis differs from that of Fixman in several ways:

(a) Inertia effects are included so that the program is applicable to both low- and high-viscosity regimes.

One of the purposes of this paper, then, is to present the linked rigid body approach to the numerical treatment of the dynamics of long-chain molecules. This is done in Sec. II. The resulting program is operational for chains of arbitrary valence angle and practical, from the viewpoint of computer time, for chains up to 15 bonds in length. Preparatory to accumulating results for long chains, we examine here some basic theoretical questions relating to chain dynamics specializing, for this purpose, the general procedure to a three-bond chain with 900 valence angles. The questions we treat are as follows:

(a) Equilibrium statistics of rigid vs flexible models: As we have indicated above, the bond lengths and valence angles in a long-chain molecule may be regarded as geometric constraints. We refer to this type of model as rigid although, of course, dihedral angle variation about the backbone is possible. On the other hand, the bond lengths and valence angles may be maintained nearly constant by the use of appropriate interatomic potentials, most simply by using harmonic potentials with large spring constants. We refer to the latter as a flexible model. There has been extensive discussion in the literature of the relationship of the equilibrium statistics of the two types of models; a summary may be found in GO and Scheraga. 7,8 The classical equilibrium statistics satisfied by the two types of models differ, with the appearance of a metric determinant in the distribution in configuration space for rigid models which is absent for flexible models no matter how large the spring constants. If attention is confined to purely classical con-Siderations, it is clear that the flexible model must be regarded as more realistic than the rigid. The question is complicated, however, by quantum considerations since at room temperature the ciegrees of freedom relating to bond stretching and bending are partially "frozen." Go and Scheraga8 consider the quantum aspects of the problem and conclude that, although both classical models introduce some error because of the. neglect of quantum effects, the flexible model is the more accurate for the calculation of equilibrium properties and therefore the metric determinant should not appear in the configuration space distribution. The finite spring constant of the flexible model is needed only to reach the conclusion that the metric determinant should be

omitted; beyond that, Go and Scheraga conclude that the model may be treated as rigid in the computation of equilibrium properties.

The fact that the distributions in configuration space of the two models differ so radically is counter to one's physical intuition, since, for large spring constants, the two models are nearly identical. The conclusion has been verified by Gottlieb and Bird9 with a molecular dynamics computation for a two-bonded molecule with variable valence angle. Here (Sec. III) we consider a Brownian dynamics computation for the three-bonded chain with 900 valence angle using both the rigid model described previously and related flexible model, both corresponding to the freely rotating case. We find, in agreement with theory, that the distribution of the single variable dihedral angle *cp* is uniform for the flexible model and nonuniform for the rigid model. Furthermore, the distribution of *cp* in the rigid model is found in the computer simulation to correspond to the square root of the metric determinant *g(cp)* for this model, again in complete accordance with the theory.

Fixman5 has suggested that the computer simulation of the rigid model can be made to reproduce the configuration space distribution of the flexible model by the introduction of a compensating potential of the form *U(cp)* = *kB T* In-./g((i)), and calculations described in Sec. III verify that this is in fact the case.

- (b) Barrier crossing rates: The conclusion of Go and Scheraga8 regarding the omission of the metric determinant was based on the comparison of the quantum partition function for the flexible model and the classical partition functions for the two types of models. Their analYSiS, therefore, applies directly only to the calculation of equilibrium properties and it appears that further study is needed regarding its omission in calculating barrier crossing rates. We do not undertake such a study here but explore the difference in barrier crOSSing rates for a three-bond chain as described by (i) the rigid model with no compensating potential, (ii) the rigid model with the compensating Fixman potential, and (iii) the flexible model. For the purpose of this comparison, we use a rotational potential with a single barrier. An interesting result is that the barrier crossing rates depend on the dihedral angular location of the barrier peak in case (i), but is independent of its location for cases (ii) and (iii).
- (c) Effect of stress on barrier crOSSing rates: We turn next in Sec. V to the study of the effect of an applied stress (i. e., equal and opposite forces fixed in space applied to the end atoms) for the rigid model with the Fixman potential. It is found that an applied stress again causes the crossing rates to depend upon the location of the barrier peak. A transition-state rate theory is developed for this process. This theory is two dimensional in nature, requiring the explicit treatment of the orientation of the molecule relative to the applied stress in addition to the variable internal dihedral angle. It is exact within the framework of the transition-state assumption and predicts an inherent curvature of the Arrhenius plots.

Agreement between the theory and computer-simulated transition-state rates is good. Since the theory is exact, this serves as a check on the accuracy of the computer simulation procedures.

#### II. THE MODEL

### A. Linked rigid body formulation

In this section, we apply the formalism of Wittenburg<sup>6</sup> to a model of the rigid chain. This approach is a very general systematic numerical method for solving the motion of a system of connected rigid bodies. Rather than use a Lagrangian which must be redeveloped from fundamentals for each system, Wittenburg starts with Newton's law and the law of moment of momentum to develop a method applicable to general systems of linked rigid bodies. The definition of the system requires (1) knowledge of the connectivity of the system, (2) a description of each rigid body, and (3) definition of the kinematical nature of the connection points, or hinges. In the case of the backbone chain, the relatively simple connectivity simplifies the application of this method to our model.

To fix notation, consider the section of a polyethylene backbone chain illustrated in Fig. 1. Positions of the carbon atoms are represented by  $C_{i-2}$ ,  $C_{i-1}$ ,  $C_i$ , and  $C_{i+1}$ . The bond vector  $\mathbf{l}_i$  is the segment  $C_{i-1}C_i$ ; the valence angle  $\theta_i$  is the angle between bond vectors  $\mathbf{l}_i$  and  $\mathbf{l}_{i-1}$ ; and the dihedral angle  $\phi_i$  measures the rotation of bond vector  $\mathbf{l}_i$  around an axis along the bond  $\mathbf{l}_{i-1}$ . The angle  $\phi_i = 0$  corresponds to a trans conformation of the backbone chain.

The rigid chain with N bonds is formed by constraining the valence angles  $\theta_i$  for  $i=2,\ldots,N$  and bond lengths  $|1_i|$  for  $i=1,\ldots,N$  to the constant values  $\theta$  and l. Only the N-2 dihedral angles  $\phi_i$  for  $i=3,\ldots,N$  are permitted to vary.

The mechanical system corresponding to the rigid chain is shown in Fig. 2. Each link of the chain consists of one backbone atom with two massless connecting rods of length l/2 representing the bonds, which form an angle of  $\theta$  with each other. These rods connect to one another to form a chain with valence angle  $\theta$  and bond length l. To terminate the chain, each of the two end atoms of the chain and its respective nearest neighbor are treated as one rigid body. The connecting

Theres, this mechanimodel eliminates variation of both bond lengths and valence angles while permitting conformation changes through variation of the dihedral angles. A torque internal to each hinge is used to produce a rotational potential governing the dihedral angle associated with that hinge.

In addition to the internal degrees of freedom, there are six additional degrees of freedom of the chain; three for the translational motion of the center of mass of the entire chain, and three angular coordinates to describe the orientation of the first body in the chain with respect to a reference frame. The three translational coordinates of the center of mass produce separate equations of motion in the Wittenburg approach and are eliminated from the formulation. Bryant angles, which are similar to Euler angles, are used for the orientational coordinates of the first body. The three Bryant angles  $\phi_0$ ,  $\phi_1$ , and  $\phi_2$  describe the orientation as a rotation of  $\phi_0$  about the x axis of the reference frame, followed by a rotation of  $\phi_1$  about the new y axis, and finally a rotation of  $\phi_2$  about the new z axis. The state of the chain is then described by N+1 angles  $\phi_0, \ldots, \phi_N$ , where  $\phi_0$ ,  $\phi_1$ , and  $\phi_2$  describe the orientation of the first body with respect to a fixed frame of reference and the dihedral angles  $\phi_3, \ldots, \phi_N$  describe the internal conformation. Since the first body orients the entire chain for a given conformation, the angles  $\phi_0$ ,  $\phi_1$ , and  $\phi_2$  describe the overall orientation of the chain.

In order to discuss the method of solution, it is necessary to introduce a number of concepts which are discussed in greater detail and generality by Wittenburg. 6 He makes extensive use of mathematical graph theory to describe the connectivity of the system. We here specialize the general discussion to the simple case of a linear chain.

(1) Connectivity: If, starting from atom  $C_0$ , a path is followed along each bond to the atom  $C_j$ , each atom  $C_i$  with i < j is in the path, while no atom  $C_k$  for k > j is included in the path. This type of connectivity is the simplest form of a tree structure in which only one path is possible between any two atoms  $C_i$  and  $C_j$ . A connectivity matrix  $\underline{T}$  is defined with  $T_{ij} = -1$  if the atom  $C_j$  is on the path between atom  $C_i$  and the body containing atoms  $C_{N-1}$  and  $C_N$ . For the chain, therefore,

$$T_{ij} = \begin{cases} -1, & j \ge i \\ 0, & i < i \end{cases}, \quad i, j = 1, \dots, N-1.$$
 (2.1)

Also, the hinge between atoms  $C_j$  and  $C_{j+1}$  is called the upper hinge of  $C_j$  and the lower hinge of  $C_{j+1}$ .

(2) Augmented bodies: The augmented body  $B_j$  for the body containing the atom  $C_j$ , referred to simply as body  $C_j$ , is formed by collapsing the atoms  $C_0, C_1, \ldots, C_{j-1}$  to a point mass at the lower hinge point and atoms  $C_{j+1} \cdot \cdot \cdot \cdot C_N$  to the upper hinge point. The body  $B_j$  has a new center of mass, called its barycenter. The moment of inertia tensor for body  $B_j$  about its barycenter is  $K_j^*$ .

For each augmented body  $B_j$ , we define three body vectors  $\mathbf{b}_{j0}$ ,  $\mathbf{b}_{jj}$ , and  $\mathbf{b}_{jN}$  to describe, respectively, the positions of the lower hinge point, of the center of mass of body  $C_j$ , and of the upper hinge point with respect to the barycenter of body  $B_j$ . For the end groups,  $\mathbf{b}_{10}$  and  $\mathbf{b}_{N-1,N}$  are zero.

(3) Kinematics of hinges: The generalized coordinates are introduced into the dynamical equations through kinematical relations. These relations define the relative motion of contiguous links of the chain in terms of the dihedral angle  $\phi_i$ . The relative angular velocity of the body  $C_i$  with respect to body  $C_{i-1}$  is

$$\Omega_i = \mathbf{p}_{i+1} \dot{\phi}_{i+1}, \quad i = 2, \dots, N-1,$$
 (2.2)

where  $p_{i+1}$  is a unit vector along the bond between  $C_{i-1}$  and  $C_i$ , which restricts rotation in the hinge to that axis, allowing the only degree of freedom for that hinge to be  $\phi_{i+1}$ .

It is convenient in the formulation to extend this notation to the first body so that

$$\Omega_1 = p_0 \dot{\phi}_0 + p_1 \dot{\phi}_1 + p_2 \dot{\phi}_2 \tag{2.3}$$

is the angular velocity of the first body in the reference frame. Here, the vectors  $\mathbf{p}_0$ ,  $\mathbf{p}_1$ , and  $\mathbf{p}_2$  are unit vectors along the axes defining the Bryant angles  $\phi_0$ ,  $\phi_1$ , and  $\phi_2$ , respectively. The absolute angular velocity for each body is then

$$\omega_i = \sum_{j=1}^i \Omega_j , \quad i = 1, \dots, N-1 .$$
 (2.4)

(4) Internal hinge torques: In addition to the constraining aspect of the hinges, the Wittenburg formulation allows interaction between bodies through the hinges. By allowing torques internal to the hinges, the rotational potential of side group interaction  $V_{\phi}$  may be incorporated into the model. For our selection of coordinates, the resulting torque acts on body  $C_{i-1}$  through the lower hinge along the corresponding vector  $\mathbf{p}_i$  with magnitude

$$\tau_i = \partial V_{\phi} / \partial \phi_i , \quad i = 3, \dots, N , \qquad (2.5)$$

and  $\tau_i = 0$  for i = 0, 1, 2.

Following the Wittenburg method for the chain results in a matrix equation of the form

$$\underline{A} \ddot{\phi} = \underline{B} \tag{2.6}$$

with

$$\underline{A} = (\mathbf{p} \, \underline{T}) \cdot \underline{K} \cdot (\underline{\mathbf{p}} \, \underline{T})^T \tag{2.7}$$

$$B = -(\mathbf{p} T) \cdot \{ \mathbf{K} \cdot (T^T \mathbf{f}) + \mathbf{M}' + \mathbf{M}_n + \mathbf{L}(t) \} - \tau , \qquad (2.8)$$

where the bar underneath the symbol denotes a matrix whose elements may have tensorial rank 0, 1, or 2 as indicated. For a chain with N bonds,  $\underline{\phi}$  is a column matrix of N+1 elements  $[\phi_0 \phi_1 \circ \circ \phi_N]^T$ ,  $\underline{A}$  is a symmetric positive definite matrix of dimension N+1, and  $\underline{B}$  a column matrix of N+1 elements.

The N+1 by N-1 matrix of vectors  $\underline{\mathbf{p}}$  is defined by its transpose

$$\underline{\mathbf{p}}^{T} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p}_{0} & \mathbf{p}_{1} & \mathbf{p}_{2} & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \mathbf{p}_{3} & & \vdots \\ \vdots & & & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \dots & \dots & \ddots & \mathbf{p}_{N} \end{bmatrix}$$
 (2.9)

and the matrix of second rank tensors K is

$$\mathbf{K}_{ij} = \begin{cases} \mathbf{K}_{i}^{*}, & j = i, \\ M[\mathbf{b}_{j0}\mathbf{b}_{iN} - (\mathbf{b}_{j0} \cdot \mathbf{b}_{iN})\mathbf{E}], & j > i, \end{cases}$$
 (2.10)

where M is the total mass of the chain and E the three-dimensional identity tensor. For  $j \le i$ , the tensor  $K_{ij}$  is the transpose of the tensor  $K_{ji}$ .

The column matrix  $\underline{\mathbf{f}}$  resulting from the kinematics of the chain has elements

$$f_{1} = \sum_{k=0}^{2} \sum_{l=0}^{2} \frac{\partial \mathbf{p}_{l}}{\partial \phi_{k}} \dot{\phi}_{k} \dot{\phi}_{l} ,$$

$$f_{i} = \omega_{t-1} \times \Omega_{i} , \quad i = 2, \dots, N-1 ,$$
(2.11)

where the partial derivatives  $\partial p_l/\partial \phi_k$  are given in Appendix A. The matrix  $\mathbf{M}'$  has elements

$$\mathbf{M}_{i}' = -\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i} \times \mathbf{K}_{i}^{*} \cdot \boldsymbol{\omega}_{i} + M \left[ \mathbf{b}_{i \, 0} \times \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \boldsymbol{\omega}_{j} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j} \times \mathbf{b}_{jN}) + \mathbf{b}_{i \, N} \times \sum_{j=i+1}^{N-1} \boldsymbol{\omega}_{j} \times (\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j} \times \mathbf{b}_{j0}) \right], \quad i = 1, \dots, N-1 \quad (2.12)$$

The matrices  $\underline{L}$  and  $\underline{M}_{\eta}$  arise from the Langevin formulation. If for each atom  $C_i$  the Langevin force is  $\underline{L}_{C_i}$ , then the form of  $\underline{L}$  for the interior links of the chain is

$$\mathbf{L}_{i} = \mathbf{b}_{i0} \times \sum_{j=0}^{i-1} \mathbf{L}_{C_{j}} + \mathbf{b}_{ii} \times \mathbf{L}_{C_{i}} + \mathbf{b}_{iN} \sum_{j=i+1}^{N} \mathbf{L}_{C_{j}},$$

$$i = 2, \dots, N-2. \qquad (2.13)$$

Because the first body contains both  $C_0$  and  $C_1$ , the form of Eq. (2.13) is altered slightly to account for the torque around the center of mass of that body so that

$$\mathbf{L}_{1} = \mathbf{b}_{11} \times (\mathbf{L}_{C_{0}} + \mathbf{L}_{C_{1}}) + \mathbf{b}_{1N} \sum_{j=2}^{N} \mathbf{L}_{C_{j}} + \rho_{0} \times \mathbf{L}_{C_{0}} + \rho_{1} \times \mathbf{L}_{C_{1}}, \qquad (2.14)$$

where  $\rho_0$  and  $\rho_1$  are vectors from the center of mass of the first body to atoms  $C_0$  and  $C_1$ , respectively. Similarly,

$$\mathbf{L}_{N-1} = \mathbf{b}_{N-1, N-1} \times (\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{C}_{N-1}} + \mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{C}_{N}}) + \mathbf{b}_{N-1, 0} \sum_{j=0}^{N-2} \mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{C}_{j}} + \boldsymbol{\rho}_{N-1} \times \mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{C}_{N-1}} + \boldsymbol{\rho}_{N} \times \mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{C}_{N}}$$
(2.15)

is the corresponding equation for the other terminating body.

To describe the torque  $\underline{\mathbf{M}}_{\eta}$ , we first consider the damping force

$$\mathbf{F}_i = -\eta \mathbf{\hat{r}}_i , \qquad (2.16)$$

where  $\eta$  is the viscosity and  $\mathbf{r}_i$  is the position vector of atom  $C_i$  relative to the center of mass of the chain. In terms of this force,

$$\mathbf{M}_{\eta_{i}} = \mathbf{b}_{i0} \times \sum_{j=0}^{i-1} \mathbf{F}_{j} + \mathbf{b}_{ii} \times \mathbf{F}_{i} + \mathbf{b}_{iN} \sum_{j=i+1}^{N} \mathbf{F}_{j} ,$$

$$i = 2, \dots, N-2 . \qquad (2.17)$$

Again, because of the form of the terminating group, somewhat different expressions result for  $\mathbf{M}_{\eta_1}$  and  $\mathbf{M}_{\eta_{N-1}}$ , namely,

$$\mathbf{M}_{\eta_{1}} = \mathbf{b}_{11} \times (\mathbf{F}_{0} + \mathbf{F}_{1}) + \mathbf{b}_{1N} \times \sum_{j=2}^{N} \mathbf{F}_{j} + \boldsymbol{\rho}_{0} \times \mathbf{F}_{0} + \boldsymbol{\rho}_{1} \times \mathbf{F}_{1}$$

$$+ \boldsymbol{\rho}_{N-1} \times \mathbf{F}_{N-1} + \boldsymbol{\rho}_{N} \times \mathbf{F}_{N},$$

$$(2.18)$$

$$\mathbf{F}_{i} = \begin{pmatrix} -\eta \left[ \sum_{j=2}^{N-1} \boldsymbol{\omega}_{j} \times \mathbf{b}_{j0} + \boldsymbol{\omega}_{1} \times (\mathbf{b}_{11} + \boldsymbol{\rho}_{i}) \right], & i = 0, 1, \\ -\eta \left( \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} \boldsymbol{\omega}_{j} \times \mathbf{b}_{jN} + \boldsymbol{\omega}_{i} \times \mathbf{b}_{ii} + \sum_{j=i+1}^{N-1} \boldsymbol{\omega}_{j} \times \mathbf{b}_{j0} \right), & i = 2, \dots, N-2, \\ -\eta \left[ \sum_{j=1}^{N-2} \boldsymbol{\omega}_{j} \times \mathbf{b}_{jN} + \boldsymbol{\omega}_{N-1} \times (\mathbf{b}_{N-1, N-1} + \boldsymbol{\rho}_{i}) \right], & i = N-1, N, \end{pmatrix}$$

The last term in Eq. (2.8)  $(\underline{\tau})$  represents the effect of the internal hinge torques, as discussed previously.

All of the tensorial quantities used to calculate  $\underline{A}$  and  $\underline{B}$  of Eq. (2.6) are independent of coordinate system. Therefore, all of the vectors and tensors associated with each body are first defined in a coordinate system attached to that body. These definitions need be made only once in that body coordinate system. Calculation of  $\underline{A}$  and  $\underline{B}$  then proceeds by transforming all vectors or tensors to a common reference frame and calculating the expressions in Eqs. (2.7) and (2.8). For the chain, we use a set of successive transformations similar to those used by Flory<sup>10</sup> in the study of chain statistics. These transformations, along with other explicit quanti-

ties necessary for the computation of Eqs. (2.7) and (2.8), are included in Appendix A.

For comparison with the rigid chain, a corresponding flexible model has also been developed in which the valence angles and bond lengths are no longer constrained to be constant, but are held nearly constant by a strong harmonic potential. The equations of motion for this model, developed in Cartesian coordinates, are incidental to our discussion and are presented in Appendix B.

#### B. Langevin force simulation

The stochastic character of the Langevin force  $\mathbf{L}(t)$  may be described in terms of its impulse  $\mathbf{B}(\delta t)$ , where

$$\mathbf{B}(\delta t) = \int_{t}^{t+\delta t} \mathbf{L}(\tau) d\tau , \qquad (2.21)$$

where  $\mathbf{B}(\delta t)$  is independent of t. The Cartesian component  $B_f(\delta t)$  of this impulse satisfies the probability distribution<sup>11</sup>

$$\rho[B_j(\delta t)] = \frac{1}{(4\pi \eta k_B T \delta t)^{1/2}} \exp\left[-\frac{|B_j(\delta t)|^2}{4\eta k_B T \delta t}\right]. \quad (2.22)$$

The method we use here for the solution of the Langevin equation is separated into two steps: First, a fourth order Runge-Kutta method is used to extend the solution from time t to  $t+\delta t$  without the Langevin force. Then, the velocities are incremented with a random impulse with the statistical characteristics described in Eq. (2.22). These two steps are repeated to produce a trajectory of desired length.

In the flexible case, this method is applied directly since the equation of motions are in Cartesian coordinates. At each time step, a new velocity is determined as

$$v_i^{(j)} = (v_i^{(j)})^{RK} + \Delta v_i^{(j)}, \quad j = 0, \dots, N, \quad i = 1, 2, 3,$$
 (2.23)

where  $(v_i^{(j)})^{RK}$  is the Runge-Kutta solution of Eq. (B6) for the Cartesian components of the velocity of atom  $C_j$ 

$$\Delta v_i^{(j)} = \frac{1}{m_i} (2\eta k_B T \delta t)^{1/2} \gamma_i^{(j)} \tag{2.24}$$

is the impulse due to the Langevin force for a temperature T. The random variable  $\gamma_i^{(I)}$  is chosen from a Gaussian distribution with a zero mean and standard deviation of unity. The resulting impulse is therefore distributed as required by Eq. (2.22).

For the rigid model, the choice of generalized coordinates requires that the Cartesian components of the Langevin impulse be transformed to velocity increments of these coordinates. To do this, a set of impulses is determined for the atoms  $C_0, \ldots, C_N$  from Eq. (2.24). These impulses are used directly in place of the Langevin forces in Eqs. (2.13)-(2.15) to calculate  $\underline{L}$ , giving an impulse matrix  $\hat{\underline{L}}$ . Now, a new velocity is calculated as

$$\dot{\phi}_i = (\dot{\phi}_i)^{RK} + \Delta \dot{\phi}_i , \quad i = 0, \dots, N , \qquad (2.25)$$

where  $\Delta \dot{\phi}_i = [\underline{A}^{-1}(-\underline{p}\underline{T} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{L}})]_i$  is the velocity increment of the generalized coordinate  $\phi_i$ .

As noted in the Introduction, this method of treating the Langevin forces numerically is an elementary one as compared to the more elaborate approaches of Fixman,  $^5$  who has used an iterative scheme in his model for polymer dynamics, and of Helfand,  $^4$  who has employed a more accurate series method which contains the above approximation as one term. These more sophisticated methods can be incorporated into the general numerical formulation of rigid body dynamics described here, and we hope to do so in future work. Here we check, by employing various values for the numerical time step  $\delta t$ , that the accuracy achieved is sufficient for the problems being studied.

#### C. The three-bond chain

As seen in Fig. 2, when N=3, no interior links appear in the rigid model. This case, which represents a chain with three backbone bonds, has a single internal degree of freedom. We also consider the possibility that the end atoms  $C_0$  and  $C_3$  have greater mass than the interior atoms  $C_1$  and  $C_2$ , with the parameter  $\alpha$  defined as the ratio of the mass of  $C_0$  to  $C_1$  and of  $C_3$  to  $C_2$ . In a very crude way, the greater end masses may be thought to model the effect of the rest of a chain upon an interior three-bond section.

In the flexible model, the reduction to three bonds also results in one dihedral angle  $\phi_3$ . However, there are five additional internal degrees of freedom due to variations of the bond lengths  $(l_1, l_2, \text{ and } l_3)$  and the valence angles  $(\theta_2 \text{ and } \theta_3)$ .

These are the simplest possible cases of both models which preserve the presence of a variable dihedral angle.

# III. EQUILIBRIUM STATISTICS IN FLEXIBLE AND RIGID MODELS

#### A. Theory

As the harmonic forces in the flexible model become stronger, the model approaches the rigid chain as a limiting case. However, the equilibrium statistics of these models are very different, i.e., the processes of taking thermal averages and letting the spring constants of the flexible model go to infinity do not commute. For the comparison in this section, we use the three-bond chain with 90° valence angles and no internal rotational potential.

For the flexible model, the equilibrium distribution function for the internal coordinates is

$$\rho(\phi_3, l_1, l_2, l_3, \theta_2, \theta_3)$$

$$= C \exp \left\{ -\frac{\beta}{2} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{3} k_i (l_i - l)^2 + \sum_{i=2}^{3} k_{\theta} (\theta_i - \theta)^2 \right] \right\} , \qquad (3.1)$$

where C is a normalization constant,  $\beta = (k_B T)^{-1}$ , and the terms in the exponential are the harmonic potentials with spring constants  $k_i$  and  $k_\theta$  for the bond lengths and valence angles, respectively. The distribution for the dihedral angle  $\phi_3$  is found by integrating over the other external coordinates. The resulting normalized distribution

$$\rho_{\mathcal{F}}(\phi_3) = 1/2\pi \tag{3.2}$$

is uniform, even if  $k_i$  and  $k_{\theta}$  become infinitely large after the integration.

For the rigid model, an additional factor appears in the distribution function. This factor is the metric determinant which arises due to the restriction of the phase space available to the rigid model and results in the distribution function

$$\rho_{\mathcal{P}}(\phi_3) = C_{\mathcal{P}}\sqrt{g(\phi_3)} , \qquad (3.3)$$

where  $g(\phi_3)$  is the metric determinant for the phase space hypersurface resulting from constraints on bond lengths and valence angles, and the normalization constant  $C_R$  is defined by

$$C_R^{-1} = \int_0^{2\pi} \sqrt{g(\phi_3)} \, d\phi_3 \ .$$
 (3.4)

A theorem for the calculation of this determinant has been presented by Fixman.  $^{12}$  For the chain with 90° valence angles, Fixman's result states that the metric determinant g can be computed from the matrix

$$H_{ij} = \sum_{r=0}^{3} \frac{2}{m_r} \left( \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial \mathbf{x}_r} \cdot \frac{\partial c_j}{\partial \mathbf{x}_r} \right), \quad i, j = 1, \dots, 5 , \qquad (3.5)$$

where  $\mathbf{x}_r$  is the Cartesian coordinate of atom  $C_r$  and  $c_i$  represents the five constrained coordinates; the bond lengths  $|\mathbf{1}_i|$  for i=1,2,3 and, for the valence angles,  $|\mathbf{1}_{i-3}+\mathbf{1}_{i-2}|$  for i=4,5. The theorem is interesting because it relates the determinant of the metric tensor which describes the unconstrained coordinates on the hypersurface with the determinant of the matrix  $H_{ij}$  of Eq. (3.5) which describes the constrained coordinates. For the three bond chain with  $90^\circ$  valence angles, this determinant is

$$g = C_{\mathbf{g}}(a + b\cos\phi + c\cos^2\phi + \cos^4\phi)$$
, (3.6)

with

$$a = (1 + 7\alpha + 15\alpha^2 + 10\alpha^3 + 2\alpha^4)/\alpha^4$$
,  
 $b = 2(1 + \alpha)/\alpha^2$ ,

and

$$c = -(3\alpha^2 + 7\alpha + 6)/\alpha^2$$

where  $C_{\ell}$  is a constant which is absorbed in the normalization calculation, and  $\alpha$  is the ratio of the mass of the end atoms to the interior atoms.

Recently, Fixman<sup>5</sup> has discussed the necessity of introducing an additional rotational potential

$$U = k_B T \ln \sqrt{g(\phi_3)} \tag{3.7}$$

to achieve a uniform distribution. Including this compensating Fixman potential in the calculation of the distribution function eliminates the metric determinant in Eq. (3.3) and results in the uniform distribution

$$\rho_{RF}(\phi_3) = 1/2\pi \ . \tag{3.8}$$

# A. Theory

In this section, we consider the effect of the distributions discussed in Sec. III on barrier crossing rates. For this study, we employ a potential with one barrier on the interval  $[-\pi,\pi]$ ;

$$V(\phi_3) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2\pi^2} k [(\phi - \phi_b) + \pi]^2, & \phi - \phi_b < -\pi/2, \\ E_b - \frac{k}{2\pi^2} (\phi - \phi_b)^2, & -\pi/2 \le \phi - \phi_b \le \pi/2, \\ \frac{1}{2\pi^2} k [(\phi - \phi_b) - \pi]^2, & \phi - \phi_b > \pi/2, \end{cases}$$

The potential is used in the three-bond chain with  $\phi_b=0$  and  $\phi_b=\pi$ . The nature of this potential is pictorially represented in Fig. 4.

Two rates of barrier crossing are considered in the computer simulation: (1) the transition-state rate in which all crossings of the barrier peak are counted, and

(2) the effective rate in which the crossing is not considered complete until the entire barrier region is crossed. The transition-state rate is a reasonable approximation to the effective rate for low viscosity but increasingly overestimates this quantity as the viscosity  $\eta$  increases. Here we limit the theoretical discussion to the transition-state rate.

The transition-state rate for the rigid model can be computed exactly from the rate expression

# V. APPLIED STRESS

#### A. Theory

If a polymer chain connects two crosslinks in a network of an amorphous polymeric solid, it is possible to apply a force to the end of the chain by stressing the network. The rate of conformational transitions is then affected by this applied force. In this initial study, we examine the effect of a force applied to the end atoms of the rigid three-bond chain on the transition-state rate. The effect of the inherent nonuniform  $\phi_3$  distribution for the rigid chain is removed in this section by use of the compensating Fixman potential (3.7).

With the forces  $\sigma$  and  $-\sigma$  applied to atoms  $C_0$  and  $C_3$ , respectively, the total potential, other than the Fixman potential, is

$$V_{\sigma} = V(\phi_3) - \sigma \cdot \mathbf{R} \tag{5.1}$$

where **R** is the end-to-end vector of the chain. We continue to use the single barrier potential of Eq. (4.1) for  $V(\phi_3)$ . If the direction of  $\sigma$  is used as a fixed orientation vector, Eq. (5.1) becomes

$$V_{\sigma}(\phi_3, \theta) = V(\phi_3) - \sigma R(\phi) \cos \theta, \qquad (5.2)$$

where  $R(\phi) = l(3 + 2\cos\phi)^{1/2}$  is the magnitude of the end-to-end distance of the three-bond 90° valence chain and  $\Theta$  is the angle between **R** and  $\sigma$ .

The rate theory governing the passage between the minima of  $V_{\sigma}(\phi,\Theta)$  requires two dimensions for its description. A transition-state rate theory in more than one dimension requires the determination of the location of the minima, the saddle point between the two minima

for which a rate is to be found, and the critical hypersurface through this saddle point. Usually, the difficulty in determining these quantities makes it necessary to introduce a number of simplifying approximations. However, the form of Eq. (5.2) is such that the minimum is unchanged from that of the potential in Eq. (4.1), the saddle point is located at  $\phi_3 = \phi_b$  and  $\Theta = 0$ , and, most importantly, the critical hypersurface remains planar. Therefore, it is possible to calculate the transition-state rate exactly for the three-bond chain.

# VI. CONCLUSIONS

In this paper, we have developed a model for the Brownian dynamics of a backbone chain of a macromolecule in which constraints on bond lengths and valence angles are satisfied exactly. The method used is a systematic formulation outlined by Wittenburg<sup>6</sup> for the dynamics of systems of connected rigid bodies. The general nature of this formulation makes it a very flexible approach to a number of problems, including the inertial effect of side groups, the dynamics of branched polymer chains, and the study of chains with increased degrees of internal flexibility, as when oxygen is present in the backbone chain. This ease of generalization makes this formalism a more valuable approach to this class of problems than the usual Lagrangian techniques.

For a comparative study of equilibrium statistics, a flexible model corresponding to the rigid chain with bond length and valence angles maintained nearly constant by strong springs was also developed. For the initial study, both of these models are specialized to the threebond chain with 90° valence angles.

For the case with no internal rotational potential, equilibrium distributions of the free internal dihedral angle as generated by computer simulation were measured for both models. As expected from theory, the flexible model showed a uniform distribution of dihedral angle  $\phi_3$ , while the rigid chain produced a nonuniform distribution proportional to the square root of the metric determinant. If a compensating Fixman potential  $U = k_B T \ln \sqrt{g(\phi)}$  is added to the internal rotational potential, a uniform distribution results in the simulation of the rigid model. This metric determinant will also arise in the simulation of longer chains, and in future work it will be important to estimate the magnitude of this effect, and, if possible, to compensate for it with an appropriate Fixman potential.

A single barrier potential was introduced so that the angular position of its barrier peak could be controlled. An exact transition-state rate formula was developed which indicated a dependence of rate on the barrier peak position for the uncompensated rigid model. This was supported by the simulation. The introduction of the Fixman potential removed this dependence on barrier position, and for the flexible case, a similar independence of barrier peak position was found. More realistic effective rates were found to behave in a qualitatively similar manner. From the standpoint of an actual physical system, it is only the effective rates of completed jumps from one well to an adjacent well which lead to observable results. The transition-state rates describing rates of crossing of a critical hypersurface separating these wells are valuable, from an experimental viewpoint, only insofar as they approximate effective rates. However, in computer simulation studies, there is no difficulty in measuring transition-state rates directly. Since the analytical formulas for the transitionstate rates developed here are exact, they serve as a check on the numerical accuracy of the method of introducing Langevin effects. As expected, it was found that results were dependent upon the size of the numerical integration time step  $\delta t$  with a rather small time step required to achieve acceptable accuracy. It is expected, in future work, that an improved method of introducing thermal effects will allow the choice of a larger integration step.

Further study of the rigid model was made with a force applied to the end atoms. Again, an exact transition state formula was developed, this time for a two dimensional rate problem. The theory predicts an inherent curvature in the Arrhenius plots which may be traced to the temperature-dependent average misorientation  $\langle\cos\Theta\rangle$  between the applied force vector  $\sigma$  and the molecule end-to-end vector  $\mathbf{R}$ , which increases with temperature. The effect of this misorientation depends on the location of the barrier peak, and so the curvature directions are opposite for  $\phi_b=0$  and  $\phi_b=\pi$ . Good agreement is found between the simulation and theory.

In this paper, we have presented a general formulation for the treatment of long-chain molecules as linked rigid bodies, but have presented numerical results only for the three-bond 90° valence angle case. These numerical results, together with corresponding analytical transition-state rate formulas, have served to clarify some questions relating to barrier crossing rates in macromolecules. We hope to apply these insights and the computer program to the study of longer chains in future work.

# APPENDIX A: DETAILS OF LINKED RIGID BODY FORMALISM

In order to calculate Eqs. (2.7) and (2.8), several of the ideas in Sec. II must be developed more explicitly. In this Appendix, we discuss (a) the body fixed coordinate systems and transformations between them, (b) the description of individual bodies, and (c) the vectors governing the degrees of freedom in a hinge.

#### 1. Coordinate transformations

A body fixed coordinate system is defined for each of the N-1 rigid bodies of the chain. We use a set of coordinate systems similar to those employed by Flory. <sup>10</sup> The coordinate system for body  $C_i$ , for  $i=1,\ldots,N-1$ , has its x axis along the bond from atom  $C_{i-1}$  to atom  $C_i$ ; the y axis is perpendicular to the x axis and in the plane of the atoms  $C_{i-1}$ ,  $C_i$ , and  $C_{i+1}$ ; and the z axis is chosen to form a right-handed coordinate system.

With this definition of the body fixed frames, a vector V with components in the coordinate system of body  $C_i$  (written as  $V^{(i)}$ ) can be expressed in terms of components in the frame of body  $C_{i-1}$  as

$$V^{(i-1)} = T(\phi_{i+1})V^{(i)}$$
, (A1)

where the matrix

$$T(\phi) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\cos\phi & \sin\theta\sin\phi \\ -\sin\theta & -\cos\theta\cos\phi & \cos\theta\sin\phi \\ 0 & -\sin\phi & -\cos\phi \end{pmatrix}. \quad (A2)$$

The transformation from the first body frame to the reference frame is accomplished with the matrix

$$a^{01} = \begin{pmatrix} c_1 c_2 & -c_1 s_2 & s_1 \\ c_0 s_2 + s_0 s_1 c_2 & c_0 c_2 - s_0 s_1 s_2 & -s_0 c_1 \\ s_0 s_2 - c_0 s_1 c_2 & s_0 c_2 + c_0 s_1 s_2 & c_0 c_1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad (A3)$$

where  $c_i = \cos \phi_i$ , and  $s_i = \sin \phi_i$ . With successive application of Eq. (A1) and the transformation of Eq. (A3), the vectors in each body frame are transformed to the reference frame.

# 11. Description of individual bodies

In the body fixed frames described above, the augmented body vectors for the interior bodies  $B_2, \ldots$ ,

 $B_{N-2}$ , are, for  $i=2,\ldots,N-2$ ,

$$\mathbf{b}_{i0} = \frac{l}{2M} [-\beta_i \cos\theta - (N + \alpha - i) \quad \beta_i \sin\theta \quad 0]^T,$$

$$\mathbf{b}_{ii} = \frac{l}{2M} [-\beta_i \cos\theta + (\alpha - 1 + i) \beta_i \sin\theta \ 0]^T, \quad (A4)$$

and

$$\mathbf{b}_{iN} = \frac{l}{2M} [(\alpha + i)\cos\theta + (\alpha - 1 + i) - (\alpha + i)\sin\theta \quad 0]^T.$$

Here, M is the total mass of the chain,  $\beta_i = N + \alpha - 1 - i$ , and  $\alpha$  is the ratio of the mass of  $C_0$  to  $C_1$ . After transforming these to the reference frame, the augmented body tensor  $K_i^*$  is calculated by

$$\mathbf{K}_{i}^{*} = (\alpha - 1 + i)(\mathbf{b}_{i0}^{2}\mathbf{E} - \mathbf{b}_{i0}\mathbf{b}_{i0}^{T}) + (\mathbf{b}_{ii}^{2}\mathbf{E} - \mathbf{b}_{ii}\mathbf{b}_{ii}^{T}) + (N + \alpha - i - 1)(\mathbf{b}_{iN}^{2}\mathbf{E} - \mathbf{b}_{iN}\mathbf{b}_{iN}^{T}) .$$
(A5)

The body vectors for the first body are

$$\mathbf{b}_{10} = 0$$

$$\mathbf{b}_{11} = \frac{-(N-2+\alpha)l}{M} \left(\frac{\cos\theta}{2} + \frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha} - \frac{\sin\theta}{2} \right)^T, \quad (A6)$$

$$\mathbf{b}_{12} = \frac{l}{M} \left( \frac{\alpha + 1}{2} \cos \theta + \alpha - \frac{\alpha + 1}{2} \sin \theta \right)^{T},$$

and the augmented inertia tensor becomes

$$\mathbf{K}_{1}^{*} = \begin{pmatrix} a & b & 0 \\ b & c & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & a+c \end{pmatrix},\tag{A7}$$

where

$$a = \frac{(N-2+\alpha)(\alpha+1)}{4M} \sin^2 \theta ,$$

$$b = \frac{N-2+\alpha}{2M} \sin\theta \left(\frac{\alpha+1}{2}\cos\theta + \alpha\right) , \tag{A8}$$

and

$$c = \frac{1}{M} \left[ (N-2+\alpha) \cos\theta \left( \frac{\alpha+1}{4} \cos\theta + \alpha \right) + \alpha (N+\alpha-1) \right].$$

Once these have been computed, corresponding values are found for the other terminating body by transforming with

$$\mathbf{R} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta & 0\\ \sin\theta & -\cos\theta & 0\\ 0 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \tag{A9}$$

so that  $\mathbf{b}_{N-1, N} = 0$ ,  $\mathbf{b}_{NN} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{b}_{11}$ ,  $\mathbf{b}_{N0} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{b}_{12}$ , and  $\mathbf{K}_{N}^{*} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{K}_{N}^{*}\mathbf{R}^{T}$ .

# APPENDIX B: DETAILS OF FLEXIBLE CHAIN FORMALISM

If  $\mathbf{x}_i = (x_1^{(i)}, x_2^{(i)}, x_3^{(i)})$  represents the position in Cartesian coordinates of the backbone atom  $C_i$  in a chain of N+1 atoms, then the generalized coordinates  $(l_i, \theta_i, \phi_i)$  discussed in Sec. II are

$$l_i = |l_i| = |\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_{i-1}|, \quad i = 1, \dots, N,$$
 (B1a)

$$\theta_i = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{l_i \cdot l_{i-1}}{l_i l_{i-1}}\right), \quad i = 2, \dots, N,$$
 (B1b)

$$\phi_{i} = \cos^{-1} \left[ -\frac{(\mathbf{l}_{i-1} \times \mathbf{l}_{i-2})}{|\mathbf{l}_{i-1} \times \mathbf{l}_{i-2}|} \cdot \frac{(\mathbf{l}_{i} \times \mathbf{l}_{i-1})}{|\mathbf{l}_{i} \times \mathbf{l}_{i-1}|} \right], \quad i = 3, \dots, N. \text{ (B1c)}$$

The 3N+3 equations of motion for the flexible chain are

$$m_{i}\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{i} = -\nabla_{i} \left[ \sum_{j=1}^{N} V_{i}(l_{j}) + V_{\theta}(\theta_{j}) \right] - \nabla_{i} V_{\phi}(\phi_{3}, \dots, \phi_{N})$$
$$- \eta \dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i} + \mathbf{L}_{i}(t) , \quad i = 0, \dots, N .$$
(B2)

Here,

$$\nabla_{i} = \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}^{(l)}}, \frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}^{(l)}}, \frac{\partial}{\partial x_{3}^{(l)}}\right),$$

$$V_{l}(l_{j}) = \frac{1}{2} k_{l}(l_{j} - l)^{2}, \quad j = 1, \dots, N,$$
(B3)

and

$$V_{\theta}(\theta_{j}) = 0$$
,  $j = 1$ ,  
=  $\frac{1}{2}k_{\theta}(\theta_{j} - \theta)^{2}$ ,  $j = 2, ..., N$ , (B4)

where  $k_i$  and  $k_{\theta}$  are spring constants, and l and  $\theta$  determine the minima around which oscillations occur. The last potential  $V_{\Phi}(\phi_3,\ldots,\phi_N)$  produces hindered internal rotation. The rotations represented by  $\phi_i$  are assumed to be independent along the chain so that

$$V_{\phi}(\phi_3, \ldots, \phi_N) = \sum_{i=3}^{N} V(\phi_i)$$
 (B5)

Note the absence of angles  $\theta_1$ ,  $\phi_1$ , and  $\phi_2$  for which Eqs. (B1b) and (B1c) are not defined. Physically, these coordinates describe the overall orientation of the chain and are therefore not governed by the internal potentials  $V_{\theta}$  and  $V_{\phi}$ . The forms  $-\eta \dot{\mathbf{x}}_i$  and  $\mathbf{L}_i(t)$  are, respectively, the frictional force and random fluctuating force of the Langevin formulation.

If the coordinates  $l_i$ ,  $\theta_i$ , and  $\phi_i$  are replaced using Eqs. (B1), and the differentiations are performed with respect to the Cartesian coordinates, then the resulting equations of motion are

$$m_{i}\ddot{\mathbf{x}}_{i} = \mathbf{F}_{i_{i}}^{i} + \mathbf{F}_{i_{i+1}}^{i} + \sum_{k=0}^{2} \mathbf{F}_{\theta_{i+k}}^{i} + \sum_{k=0}^{3} \mathbf{F}_{\phi_{i+k}}^{i} - \eta \dot{\mathbf{x}}_{i} + \mathbf{L}_{i}(t) , \quad i = 0, \dots, N ,$$
 (B6)

where  $\mathbf{F}_{\alpha_k}^{j}$  is the force on the atom  $C_j$  due to the presence

of the Cartesian coordinate  $\mathbf{x}_{j}$  in the generalized coordinate  $\alpha_{k}$ . For example, the generalized coordinate  $\theta_{k}$  depends upon  $\mathbf{x}_{k-2}$ ,  $\mathbf{x}_{k-1}$ , and  $\mathbf{x}_{k}$  so the potential involving  $\theta_{k}$  produces a force on the atoms  $C_{k-2}$ ,  $C_{k-1}$ , and  $C_{k}$ , respectively. The force  $\mathbf{F}_{\alpha_{k}}^{j}$  is zero if the generalized coordinate  $\alpha_{k}$  is not defined for that value of k, e.g.,  $\mathbf{F}_{l_{0}}^{0} = 0$  since  $\mathbf{I}_{0}$  is undefined.

The bond stretching forces  $\mathbf{F}_{lb}^{j}$  are

$$\mathbf{F}_{l_i}^i = -\,k_l \mathbf{l}_i (1-l/l_i) \;, \quad i=1,\ldots,N \;,$$
 and

$$\mathbf{F}_{l+1}^{i} = k_{l} \mathbf{I}_{l+1} (1 - l/l_{l+1}), \quad i = 1, \dots, N-1,$$

where l is the length of an undeformed bond. The forces  $\mathbf{F}_{\theta_k}^{l}$  governing the vibration of the valence angles are

$$\begin{split} \mathbf{F}_{\theta_{i}}^{i} &= -K_{\theta_{i}}(\mathbf{l}_{i-1} - d_{i}^{i-1}\mathbf{e}_{i}) , \quad i = 2, \ldots, N , \\ \mathbf{F}_{\theta_{i+1}}^{i} &= -K_{\theta_{i+1}}[\mathbf{l}_{i+1} - \mathbf{l}_{i} + d_{i}^{i+1}(\mathbf{e}_{i+1} - \mathbf{e}_{i})] , \\ & \qquad \qquad i = 2, \ldots, N-1 , \quad (B8) \end{split}$$

$$\mathbf{F}_{\theta_{i+2}}^{i} = K_{\theta_{i+2}}(\mathbf{l}_{i+2} - d_{i+1}^{i+2} \mathbf{e}_{i+2}) \ , \quad i = 2, \ldots, N-2 \ ,$$

with

$$K_{\theta_j} = \frac{k_{\theta}}{|\mathbf{1}_j \times \mathbf{1}_{j-1}|} (\theta_j - \theta) .$$

Here we use

$$\mathbf{e}_{j} \equiv \mathbf{1}_{j} / l_{j}^{2} , \qquad (B9)$$

and

$$d_{j}^{k} = d_{k}^{j} \equiv \mathbf{l}_{j} \cdot \mathbf{l}_{k} . \tag{B10}$$

Finally, the forces due to the rotational potential  $V_{\phi}$  are more compactly expressed if we use, in addition to Eq. (B10),

$$a_{j}^{k} = \left[l_{j}^{2} l_{k}^{2} - \left(d_{j}^{k}\right)^{2}\right] \tag{B11}$$

and

$${}^{i}A_{b}^{j} = (d_{i}^{i}l_{j} - l_{i}^{2}l_{i})(d_{i}^{k}l_{j}^{2} - d_{j}^{k}d_{i}^{j})(a_{i}^{j})^{-1} + (l_{i}^{2}l_{b} - d_{j}^{k}l_{j}), \quad (B12)$$

With Eqs. (B11) and (B12), the forces  $\mathbf{F}_{\phi_i}^i$  are

$$\mathbf{F}_{\phi_{i+1}}^{i} = K_{\phi_{i}}^{i} \mathbf{A}_{i-2}^{i-1}, \quad i = 3, \dots, N,$$

$$\mathbf{F}_{\phi_{i+1}}^{i} = K_{\phi_{i+1}}^{i} ({}^{i} \mathbf{A}_{i+1}^{i-1} - {}^{i+1} \mathbf{A}_{i-1}^{i} + {}^{i} \mathbf{A}_{i-1}^{i+1}), \quad i = 3, \dots, N-1,$$
(B13)

$$\mathbf{F}_{\phi_{i+2}}^{i} = -K_{\phi_{i+2}}(^{i+1}\mathbf{A}_{i}^{i+2} - ^{i}\mathbf{A}_{i+2}^{i+1} + ^{i+1}\mathbf{A}_{i+2}^{i}) \ , \quad i=3,\,\ldots,\,N-2 \ ,$$
 and

$$\mathbf{F}_{\phi_{i+3}}^{i} = -K_{\phi_{i+3}}^{i+1} \mathbf{A}_{i+3}^{i+2}, \quad i=3,\ldots,N-3.$$

The equations of motion (B6) in conjunction with Eqs. (B7), (B8), and (B13) are numerically simulated as described in Sec. IIB.
