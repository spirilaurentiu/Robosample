## **Generalized coordinate hybrid Monte Carlo**

By B. M. FORREST and U. W. SUTER Institut f/Jr Polymere, Eidgen6ssische Technische Hochschule (ETH), CH-8092 Z/irich, Switzerland

*(Received 13 September 1993; revised version accepted 15 December 1993)* 

We describe a novel hybrid Monte Carlo algorithm for the off-lattice simulation of dense, atomistically detailed polymer systems. Bond lengths and bond angles are constrained to their equilibrium values and a generalizedcoordinate description using the torsional degrees of freedom is employed. Simple, decoupled, Cartesian-like equations of motion are obtained by the introduction of fictitious angular momenta variables conjugate to the torsional degrees of freedom. A unique time scale, and hence a mapping onto real time, can be obtained by allocating time-independent, effective moments of inertia to each angular momentum variable.

#### **I. Introduction**

A prerequisite for any efficient thermalization algorithm is the ability to generate a large set of statistically independent configurations characteristic of a given constant temperature. In off-lattice simulations of atomistically detailed models both molecular dynamics (MD) and Monte Carlo (MC) algorithms attempt to generate such a sequence of representative snapshots by making suitable changes in the configuration at hand [1]. For dense polymer systems the rate at which these sample configurations can be altered is limited by the connectivity of the constituent chain molecules, which exacerbates the restrictions on movement imposed by the lack of free volume at high densities. This inevitably results in correlations between successive samples, and the efficiency of the respective methods depends on how rapidly they can erase these correlations.

The brute force approach adopted by MD of time-discretizing Newton's equations of motion is limited primarily by the presence of the high-frequency motions associated with bond length and bond angle stretching potentials. An upper bound of typically at most a few femtoseconds is placed on the time-step employed in order to retain a reasonable degree of accuracy and to avoid numerical instabilities after long times. This renders simulation times beyond the nanosecond range computationally impractical. For this reason many simulation techniques eliminate these highest-frequency motions by constraining the bond lengths and bond angles to their equilibrium values during motion I-2, 3]. However, the computational gain is restricted by the additional problem of ensuring convergence of the associated constraint equations to within an acceptable degree of tolerance [4]. An alternative to introducing additional constraint equations is to employ a generalized-coordinate description in terms of the torsional degrees of freedom along the chain. Although this is a more natural choice of coordinate system when using fixed bond lengths and bond angles and leads to at least a threefold (and usually to an order-ofmagnitude) reduction in the number of degrees of freedom, it does so at the expense

of a considerably more complicated set of equations of motion which requires matrix inversion techniques [5, 6].

MC methods make random changes in the polymer configuration. However, in order to guarantee appreciable acceptance rates, conventional MC moves alter only a few degrees of freedom at a time. Usually, displacements in one or a few units of a single chain are proposed due to the prohibitively low acceptance rates typically incurred when attempting to change all of the coordinates simultaneously. The disadvantage of making such local changes between MC steps is that the ensuing autoco~relation times can become very large, especially at high densities, implying both a slow approach to equilibrium and magnified statistical errors in measured observables. Recently, significant progress has been made in improving the efficiency and scope of single-chain MC algorithms by the introduction of more sophisticated and elegant multi-monomer moves. Continuum configurational bias [7], a cut-andregrow technique, enhances the mobility of chain ends, while concerted rotation [8], which simultaneously rotates several consecutive segments within a chain, improves the internal motion of the molecules. These algorithms, and composites thereof [9], have been shown to be capable of equilibrating melts of relatively long polybead chains (up to 40 backbone carbon atoms), although their performance was found to deteriorate considerably upon the introduction of explicit hydrogen atoms. Despite these advances, any significant breakthrough in the simulation of equilibrated polymer melts is most likely to come from global-update algorithms, which can successfully incorporate cooperative motion, not merely on the scale of individual polymers, but rather on the scale of the entire system.

Below we present a novel hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC) algorithm which incorporates the advantages of MD, while retaining those of MC. Like MD, it is a global-update algorithm but, being an MC method, it is capable of providing the partition function in configuration space and is not prone to numerical instabilities or systematic discretization errors. While the application of the HMC method has recently been advocated in off-lattice polymer simulation using Cartesian coordinates [10], our approach uses *fixed* bond lengths and bond angles within a *oeneralizedcoordinate* framework, and does so while avoiding the computational complications such a scheme would usually imply.

#### **2. The hybrid Monte Carlo algorithm**

The HMC algorithm was originally proposed as a canonical, global-update method for lattice gauge theories [11] and has since then been shown to be suitable in the broader context of condensed matter simulations [12]. Global changes are made between MC acceptance decisions by employing an MD integration for a specified number of MD steps. The MD integration at the heart of the HMC algorithm is realized by introducing momenta conjugate to the coordinate variables of the system. The corresponding set of Hamilton's equation of motion is then discretized in time and integrated for a specified number of steps, thus propelling the system through configuration space. As the momenta are directly influenced by the forces during the MD integration, the coordinates tend to evolve in the direction of decreasing energy, thus leading to a higher likelihood of accepted moves. Nevertheless, since the initial momenta are drawn at random, 'uphill' moves in energy can also be proposed. The momenta are refreshed (i.e., reset to arbitrary initial values usually drawn from a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution) after every MC acceptance decision,

regardless of whether the proposed move has been accepted or not (otherwise detailed balance cannot be satisfied). In condensed-matter systems these momenta are usually taken to be the actual momenta conjugate to the position variables, but this need not be the case. In general, one is free to introduce 'fictitious' momenta which merely serve as a means for propagating the coordinates through configuration space. This more general approach was adopted in the seminal implementation of HMC [11] and, in fact, is used in the algorithm presented here.

It is questionable whether particular forms of MD (such as velocity rescaling [13]) constitute canonical algorithms, since the condition of constant temperature, in the rigorous sense of a canonical ensemble, can be treated only approximately (however, the Nos6-Hoover thermostat [14, 15] has come to be widely accepted as a canonical MD method). The HMC method retains the advantage of global updates provided through MD integration, but it does so without in any way compromising the numerical stability and exactness inherent in MC algorithms. Unlike MD or Langevin integrations, the HMC method is both numerically stable and exact, irrespective of the size of the chosen integration time-step. It is exact in the sense that it can reproduce unbiased, canonical averages, whereas MD or Langevin dynamics produce step-size-dependent observables, which should, in principle, be extrapolated to vanishingly small time-steps. As has been pointed out already [16], the one-step version of the HMC algorithm is equivalent to an exact Langevin integration with respect to obtaining equilibrium observables. The discretization error produced in the HMC scheme by the underlying MD integration is properly accounted for in the MC acceptance criterion and has a bearing only on the acceptance rate.

#### **6. Optimizing the algorithm**

#### 7. Conclusion

In summary, we have introduced a novel hybrid Monte Carlo algorithm for the off-lattice simulation of dense, atomistically detailed polymer systems. It employs fixed bond lengths and bond angles within a generalized coordinate framework. The additional complications of coupled equations of motion normally associated with such a coordinate system are circumvented by the introduction of auxiliary angular momenta variables conjugate to the torsional degrees of freedom. This is achieved by augmenting the internal energy with a fictitious kinetic energy in the form of a simple, Cartesian-like, quadratic sum with constant coefficients, which can be interpreted as effective moments of inertia. It is shown that instantaneous moments of inertia can be associated with each angular degree of freedom and that their average values are time independent to within a good approximation in equilibrium. If these mean, instantaneous moments of inertia are chosen for the effective moments of inertia in the fictitious kinetic energy then a unique time scale is recovered, allowing mapping from computational time to real time.

Future work [23] will involve a comparison of the algorithm presented here with currently existing algorithms to ascertain its efficiency. In particular, a comparison should be made with true dynamical algorithms, such as MD, in order to assess the dynamical properties of the algorithm.
