# Hamiltonian Monte Carlo Without Detailed Balance

Jascha Sohl-Dickstein, Mayur Mudigonda, Michael R. DeWeese. ICML 2014 (JMLR W&CP vol. 32).

## Abstract

We present a method for performing Hamiltonian Monte Carlo that largely
eliminates sample rejection for typical hyperparameters. In situations that
would normally lead to rejection, instead a longer trajectory is computed until
a new state is reached that can be accepted. This is achieved using Markov chain
transitions that satisfy the fixed point equation, but do not satisfy detailed
balance. The resulting algorithm significantly suppresses the random walk
behavior and wasted function evaluations that are typically the consequence of
update rejection. We demonstrate a greater than factor of two improvement in
mixing time on three test problems. Source is released as Python and MATLAB
packages (http://github.com/Sohl-Dickstein/LAHMC).

## 1. Introduction

Sampling from a target distribution is typically the bottleneck when working
with high-dimensional probabilistic models. The most common way to guarantee a
sampler converges to the correct distribution is via **detailed balance**, which
allows samples from any target to be generated from almost any proposal using
Metropolis-Hastings acceptance. However, detailed balance has a critical flaw:
by definition forward and reverse transitions occur with equal probability, so
samplers that obey it go backwards as often as forwards. The state space is thus
explored via a random walk, and the number of steps to traverse it is quadratic
in the size of the state space. Samplers that violate detailed balance can mix
more rapidly.

The current state-of-the-art sampler for continuous state spaces is Hamiltonian
Monte Carlo (HMC). By extending the state space with auxiliary momentum
variables and using Hamiltonian dynamics to traverse long iso-probability
contours, HMC moves long distances in a single update step. However, HMC still
relies on detailed balance to accept/reject steps, and so still behaves like a
random walk with a longer step length.

This paper presents a novel discrete representation of the HMC state space and
transitions, and derives a method for performing HMC without relying on detailed
balance, by directly satisfying the fixed point equation restricted to the
discrete state space. Random walk behavior is greatly reduced and the mixing
rate substantially improved.

## 2. Sampling

The goal is to draw characteristic samples $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ from a
target distribution $p(\mathbf{x})$. Without loss of generality, $p(\mathbf{x})$
is determined by an energy function $E(\mathbf{x})$:

<!-- eq:1 -->
$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{Z} \exp(-E(\mathbf{x})).$$

### 2.1. Markov Chain Monte Carlo

In MCMC a chain of samples is generated by repeatedly drawing new samples
$\mathbf{x}'$ from a conditional distribution $T(\mathbf{x}'|\mathbf{x})$, where
$\mathbf{x}$ is the previous sample. Since $T(\mathbf{x}'|\mathbf{x})$ is a
probability density over $\mathbf{x}'$, $\int T(\mathbf{x}'|\mathbf{x})\, d\mathbf{x}' = 1$
and $T(\mathbf{x}'|\mathbf{x}) \ge 0$.

### 2.2. Fixed Point Equation

An MCMC algorithm must satisfy two conditions. The first is **mixing**: repeated
application of $T$ must eventually explore the full state space. The second is
that the target $p(\mathbf{x})$ must be a **fixed point** of $T$:

<!-- eq:2 -->
$$\int p(\mathbf{x})\, T(\mathbf{x}'|\mathbf{x})\, d\mathbf{x} = p(\mathbf{x}').$$

### 2.3. Detailed Balance

Detailed balance is the most common way of guaranteeing $T$ satisfies the fixed
point equation. It requires that, at equilibrium, for every pair of states the
probability of transitioning $\mathbf{x} \to \mathbf{x}'$ equals that of
$\mathbf{x}' \to \mathbf{x}$:

<!-- eq:3 -->
$$p(\mathbf{x})\, T(\mathbf{x}'|\mathbf{x}) = p(\mathbf{x}')\, T(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{x}').$$

By substitution into Equation 2, if Equation 3 holds then the fixed point
equation also holds. A transition satisfying detailed balance is easily
constructed from nearly any proposal using Metropolis-Hastings rules. Its
drawback: the resulting Markov chains always engage in random walk behavior.

## 3. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo

HMC traverses long distances by extending the state space with auxiliary
momentum variables and simulating Hamiltonian dynamics along iso-probability
contours.

### 3.1. Extended state space

Momentum variables $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ are added with an
identity-covariance Gaussian distribution:

<!-- eq:4 -->
$$p(\mathbf{v}) = (2\pi)^{-\frac{N}{2}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v}\right).$$

The combined state is $\zeta = \{\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}\}$, with joint
distribution

<!-- eq:5 -->
$$p(\zeta) = p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}) = p(\mathbf{x})\, p(\mathbf{v}) = \frac{(2\pi)^{-\frac{N}{2}}}{Z} \exp(-H(\zeta)),$$

<!-- eq:6 -->
$$H(\zeta) = H(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}) = E(\mathbf{x}) + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{v}.$$

$H(\zeta)$ has the same form as total energy in a physical system, where
$E(\mathbf{x})$ is the potential energy and $\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{v}$ is
the kinetic energy (mass is set to one). Samples from $p(\mathbf{x})$ are
generated by drawing from the joint $p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v})$ and retaining
only the $\mathbf{x}$ variables.

### 3.2. Hamiltonian dynamics

Running Hamiltonian dynamics traverses long distances in $\mathbf{x}$ while
maintaining constant total energy $H(\zeta)$; by Equation 5 constant energy is
constant probability density. Hamiltonian dynamics can be run exactly in reverse
by reversing the velocity vector, and they preserve volume in $\zeta$. Together
these properties allow proposals that move long distances while retaining high
acceptance probability.

### 3.3. Operators

HMC's Markov transitions are built from several operators acting on $\zeta$.

**3.3.1. Momentum Flip.** The operator $\mathbf{F}$ reverses the momentum. It is
its own inverse, leaves total energy unchanged, and preserves volume:

<!-- eq:7 -->
$$\mathbf{F}\zeta = \mathbf{F}\{\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}\} = \{\mathbf{x}, -\mathbf{v}\},$$

<!-- eq:8 -->
$$\mathbf{F}^{-1}\zeta = \mathbf{F}\zeta,$$

<!-- eq:9 -->
$$H(\mathbf{F}\zeta) = H(\zeta),$$

<!-- eq:10 -->
$$\left| \det \left( \frac{\partial \mathbf{F}\zeta}{\partial \zeta^T} \right) \right| = 1.$$

Note (Fig 1): the base of each arrow is position $\mathbf{x}$; length/direction
is momentum $\mathbf{v}$. $\mathbf{F}$ causes movement between the left and right
sides of the state "ladder".

**3.3.2. Leapfrog Integrator.** Leapfrog (Störmer-Verlet) integration provides a
discrete-time approximation to Hamiltonian dynamics. The operator
$\mathbf{L}(\epsilon, M)$ performs leapfrog integration for $M$ steps with step
length $\epsilon$; abbreviated $\mathbf{L}$:

<!-- eq:11 -->
$$\mathbf{L}\zeta = \text{state after } M \text{ leapfrog steps of Hamiltonian dynamics with step length } \epsilon.$$

Leapfrog dynamics are exactly reversible by reversing the velocity, and exactly
preserve volume. $\mathbf{L}$ is inverted by reversing the momentum sign, tracing
the reverse trajectory, and reversing the momentum sign again:

<!-- eq:12 -->
$$\mathbf{L}^{-1}\zeta = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{F}\zeta,$$

<!-- eq:13 -->
$$\left| \det \left( \frac{\partial \mathbf{L}\zeta}{\partial \zeta^T} \right) \right| = 1.$$

Unlike exact dynamics, $H(\zeta)$ is only approximately conserved by leapfrog;
discretization error in the energy is the source of all rejections in HMC.
$\mathbf{L}$ causes movement up the right side of the ladder and down the left.

**3.3.3. Momentum Randomization.** The operator $\mathbf{R}(\beta)$ mixes
Gaussian noise, controlled by $\beta \in [0,1]$, into the velocity:

<!-- eq:14 -->
$$\mathbf{R}(\beta)\zeta = \mathbf{R}(\beta)\{\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}\} = \{\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{v}'\},$$

<!-- eq:15 -->
$$\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{v}\sqrt{1-\beta} + \mathbf{n}\sqrt{\beta},$$

<!-- eq:16 -->
$$\mathbf{n} \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}).$$

$\mathbf{R}(\beta)$ is not deterministic but is a valid Markov transition
operator for $p(\zeta)$ on its own (satisfies both Equation 2 and Equation 3). It
moves off the current ladder onto a new ladder.

### 3.4. Discrete State Space

The operators $\mathbf{L}$ and $\mathbf{F}$ generate a discrete state-space
"ladder", with transitions only occurring between $\zeta$ and three other
states. Every ladder state can be written many ways, e.g.
$\mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{F}\zeta = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}\zeta = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}\zeta = \cdots$.
This discrete view lets Equation 2 be solved directly, replacing the integral
over all states with a short sum.

### 3.5. Standard HMC

HMC as typically implemented consists of the following steps. $\zeta^{(t,s)}$ is
the state at sampling step $t$, substep $s$. Each numbered item is a valid Markov
transition satisfying detailed balance; a full step is their composition.

1. (a) Generate a proposed update:

<!-- eq:17 -->
$$\zeta' = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}\zeta^{(t,0)}.$$

   (b) Accept or reject with Metropolis-Hastings:

<!-- eq:18 -->
$$\pi_{accept} = \min\left(1, \frac{p(\zeta')}{p(\zeta)}\right),$$

<!-- eq:19 -->
$$\zeta^{(t,1)} = \begin{cases} \zeta' & \text{with probability } \pi_{accept} \\ \zeta^{(t,0)} & \text{with probability } 1 - \pi_{accept} \end{cases}$$

   Since the transition $\mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}$ is its own inverse, the forward and
   reverse proposal probabilities cancel in Equation 18. On rejection, the
   computation in Equation 17 is discarded (not true in LAHMC).

2. Flip the momentum:

<!-- eq:20 -->
$$\zeta^{(t,2)} = \mathbf{F}\zeta^{(t,1)}.$$

   If Step 1 was accepted, this moves back to the right side of the ladder and
   prevents doubling back. If rejected, it causes the trajectory to double back
   on itself — wasteful, and the distance before doubling back is the
   characteristic length beyond which HMC explores by random walk.

3. Corrupt the momentum with noise:

<!-- eq:21 -->
$$\zeta^{(t+1,0)} = \mathbf{R}(\beta)\zeta^{(t,2)}.$$

   It is common to set $\beta = 1$ (full randomization each step). Smaller
   $\beta$ produced large improvements in mixing time in the experiments. One
   effective way to choose $\beta$ randomizes a fixed fraction $\alpha$ of the
   momentum per unit simulation time:

<!-- eq:22 -->
$$\beta = \alpha^{\frac{1}{\epsilon M}}.$$

## 4. Look Ahead HMC (LAHMC)

LAHMC uses Markov transitions that do not obey detailed balance but still
satisfy the fixed point equation. It eliminates much of the momentum flipping
that occurs on rejection in HMC, reducing random walk behavior, and prevents
discarded trajectory computations.

### 4.1. Intuition

In situations that would correspond to a rejection in Step 1 of Section 3.5,
LAHMC instead attempts to travel farther by applying $\mathbf{L}$ additional
times. The net effect of Steps 1–2 of standard HMC is to transition from $\zeta$
into either $\mathbf{L}\zeta$ (accept) or $\mathbf{F}\zeta$ (reject). We wish to
minimize transitions into $\mathbf{F}\zeta$: replace as many $\zeta \to
\mathbf{F}\zeta$ transitions as possible with $\zeta \to \mathbf{L}^2\zeta$. The
changes in incoming transitions are exactly counteracted because the state
$\mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}^2\zeta$ is symmetrically modified. Any remaining
$\zeta \leftrightarrow \mathbf{F}\zeta$ transitions are further minimized by
repeating with $\mathbf{L}^3\zeta$, $\mathbf{L}^4\zeta$, ..., up to a maximum
number of leapfrog applications $K$.

### 4.2. Algorithm

LAHMC consists of two steps:

1. Transition to a new state by applying $\mathbf{L}$ between 1 and $K \in
   \mathbb{Z}^+$ times, or applying $\mathbf{F}$:

<!-- eq:23 -->
$$\zeta^{(t,1)} = \begin{cases} \mathbf{L}\zeta^{(t,0)} & \text{w.p. } \pi_{\mathbf{L}^1}(\zeta^{(t,0)}) \\ \mathbf{L}^2\zeta^{(t,0)} & \text{w.p. } \pi_{\mathbf{L}^2}(\zeta^{(t,0)}) \\ \cdots & \\ \mathbf{L}^K\zeta^{(t,0)} & \text{w.p. } \pi_{\mathbf{L}^K}(\zeta^{(t,0)}) \\ \mathbf{F}\zeta^{(t,0)} & \text{w.p. } \pi_{\mathbf{F}}(\zeta^{(t,0)}) \end{cases}$$

   There is no longer a Metropolis-Hastings accept/reject step; Equation 23 is a
   valid Markov transition for $p(\zeta)$ on its own.

2. Corrupt the momentum with noise, as in Equation 21:

<!-- eq:24 -->
$$\zeta^{(t+1,0)} = \mathbf{R}(\beta)\zeta^{(t,1)}.$$

### 4.3. Transition Probabilities

The probabilities $\pi_{\mathbf{L}^a}(\zeta)$ for the leapfrog transitions from
state $\zeta$ to $\mathbf{L}^a\zeta$ are chosen greedily:

<!-- eq:25 -->
$$\pi_{\mathbf{L}^{a}}(\zeta) = \min\left[ 1 - \sum_{b < a} \pi_{\mathbf{L}^{b}}(\zeta),\; \frac{p(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}^{a}\zeta)}{p(\zeta)} \left( 1 - \sum_{b < a} \pi_{\mathbf{L}^{b}}(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}^{a}\zeta) \right) \right].$$

Equation 25 sets $\pi_{\mathbf{L}^a}(\zeta)$ as large as possible subject to the
total transition probability out of $\zeta$ not exceeding 1, and the forward
transition rate ($\zeta \to \mathbf{L}^a\zeta$) not exceeding the reverse rate
($\mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}^a\zeta \to \mathbf{F}\zeta$). Any remaining unassigned
probability is given to the momentum flip:

<!-- eq:27 -->
$$\pi_{\mathbf{F}}(\zeta) = 1 - \sum_{a} \pi_{\mathbf{L}^{a}}(\zeta).$$

Transitions are performed greedily: it is only necessary to compute
$\mathbf{L}^a\zeta$ and $\pi_{\mathbf{L}^a}(\zeta)$ if none of the transitions to
$\mathbf{L}^b\zeta$ for $b < a$ have been taken.

### 4.4. Fixed Point Equation

The transition rates satisfy **generalized detailed balance**, which follows
from the pairwise relation

<!-- eq:26 -->
$$p(\zeta)\, \pi_{\mathbf{L}^{a}}(\zeta) = p(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}^{a}\zeta)\, \pi_{\mathbf{L}^{a}}(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}^{a}\zeta).$$

### Derivation (not implemented)

Substituting the transition rates into the left side of Equation 2 and verifying
the fixed point equation. The integral over all states becomes a sum over source
states from which transitions into $\zeta$ might be initiated:

$$\int d\zeta'\, p(\zeta')\, T(\zeta|\zeta') = \int d\zeta'\, p(\zeta') \left( \sum_{a} \pi_{\mathbf{L}^{a}}(\zeta')\, \delta(\zeta - \mathbf{L}^{a}\zeta') + \pi_{\mathbf{F}}(\zeta')\, \delta(\zeta - \mathbf{F}\zeta') \right)$$

$$= \sum_{a} p(\mathbf{L}^{-a}\zeta)\, \pi_{\mathbf{L}^{a}}(\mathbf{L}^{-a}\zeta) + p(\mathbf{F}^{-1}\zeta)\, \pi_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{F}^{-1}\zeta)$$

$$= \sum_{a} p(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}^{a}\mathbf{F}\zeta)\, \pi_{\mathbf{L}^{a}}(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}^{a}\mathbf{F}\zeta) + p(\mathbf{F}\zeta)\, \pi_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{F}\zeta)$$

$$= \sum_{a} p(\mathbf{F}\zeta)\, \pi_{\mathbf{L}^{a}}(\mathbf{F}\zeta) + p(\mathbf{F}\zeta)\, \pi_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{F}\zeta)$$

$$= p(\mathbf{F}\zeta) \left[ \sum_{a} \pi_{\mathbf{L}^{a}}(\mathbf{F}\zeta) + \pi_{\mathbf{F}}(\mathbf{F}\zeta) \right] = p(\zeta).$$

Here the step $\mathbf{L}^{-a}\zeta = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{L}^{a}\mathbf{F}\zeta$
(from Equation 12) and Equation 26 are used, and the final bracket sums to 1
since the total outgoing probability from $\mathbf{F}\zeta$ is 1. Since
$p(\mathbf{F}\zeta) = p(\zeta)$ (Equation 9), the fixed point equation holds.

## 5. Experimental Results

HMC and LAHMC both used step length $\epsilon = 1$ and $M = 10$ leapfrog steps.
$\beta$ was 1 or 0.1. For LAHMC the maximum number of leapfrog applications was
$K = 4$. In all cases LAHMC outperformed standard HMC for the same
hyperparameters, often by more than a factor of 2.

The first two target distributions are 2- and 100-dimensional ill-conditioned
Gaussians; the eigenvalues of the covariance are log-linearly distributed
between 1 and $10^6$.

The third target has energy function (isotropic quadratic plus sinusoids):

<!-- eq:34 -->
$$E(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{2\sigma_1^2}\left(x_1^2 + x_2^2\right) + \cos\left(\frac{\pi x_1}{\sigma_2}\right) + \cos\left(\frac{\pi x_2}{\sigma_2}\right),$$

with $\sigma_1 = 100$ and $\sigma_2 = 2$. Although well conditioned, the sinusoids
create a "rough" surface, so traversing the quadratic well while maintaining
reasonable discretization error requires many leapfrog steps.

The majority of momentum flips in standard HMC were eliminated by LAHMC (Table
1). The acceptance rate for HMC with these hyperparameters is reasonably close to
its optimal value of 65% (Neal, 2010). Grid searches over hyperparameters showed
LAHMC outperforms standard HMC for all explored settings (with covariance
eigenvalues 1 and $10^5$ in that figure).

## 6. Future Directions

Complementary variations that could be combined: Riemann manifold HMC,
quasi-Newton HMC, Hilbert space HMC, shadow Hamiltonian methods, parameter
adaptation, Hamiltonian annealed importance sampling, split HMC, and tempered
trajectories.

Any deterministic, reversible, discrete-stepped trajectory through a state space
can be mapped onto the ladder structure. The Markov transition rules here could
therefore apply broadly. All that is required beyond the mapping is an auxiliary
variable indicating direction along the trajectory. In HMC the momentum doubles
as the direction indicator, but there could just as easily be an additional
variable $d \in \{-1, 1\}$, $p(d=1) = \frac{1}{2}$. Efficiency then depends only
on choosing a sensible, approximately energy-conserving trajectory.
